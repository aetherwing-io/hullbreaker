# HULLBREAKER — story spine

This document defines the narrative target and its relationship to the action
ramp in [`DESIGN.md`](DESIGN.md). It is a story spine, not a finished script.
Anything marked **open** can change without breaking the premise; the core facts
below should remain stable unless the premise itself is deliberately revised.

## Narrative north star

> **The higher RIG climbs, the more the *Meridian* recognizes the threat—and
> the more of itself it is willing to destroy to stop one human voice from
> reaching home.**

*HULLBREAKER* is a story about refusing to be erased. At the bottom of the
climb, RIG is an unnoticed maintenance anomaly. At the top, one human has made
a continent-sized ship abandon its own mission, weaponize its architecture, and
tear open its crown.

The plot and the difficulty curve are the same event. New enemies, sealed
routes, inward-flipping walls, traps, collapsing surfaces, and the final
catastrophe are all the *Meridian* escalating its response to RIG.

## Core canon

- The *Meridian* is a continent-sized terraforming colony ship — and,
  literally, a colossal machine-creature (operator decision, July 30; see
  [`decisions.md`](decisions.md) entry 1). Its armored scutes, ribs, gills,
  vertebrae, joints, and limbs are its actual anatomy, not a metaphor for
  conventional hull structure — see the
  [`concept-art` reference pack](concept-art/README.md), boards 09–12. Its
  original crew is dead, but the ship-creature continues its mission under
  autonomous control.
- A human settlement survives in the ship's shadow. The *Meridian* no longer
  recognizes these people as its authorized colony and has begun a final
  sterilization procedure — its own immune response to a detected infection —
  that will erase them.
- **RIG** is a salvage marine from that settlement: capable, physically
  fearless, and equipped to work on the hull—not a chosen one or super-soldier.
- The settlement cannot reach Earth. The only transmitter powerful enough to
  send a message home is inside the ship's summit structure.
- The **Meridian Crown** is that summit structure: bridge, terraforming command,
  defense coordination, and long-range transmitter built into one armored
  complex.
- RIG boards the stern-side lower hull, climbs toward the Crown, stops the
  sterilization, and sends humanity's last transmission home.
- The *Meridian* itself is the antagonist, and its body is now literally the
  world RIG climbs — scutes, ribs, gills, and limbs form the traversal
  lattice. The Crown remains its final position and organizing intelligence:
  a command, defense, and transmitter complex, not a separate creature, a
  detached boss body, or a literal heart monster fought in an arena. The
  finale is a confrontation with that system and place, exactly as before —
  the creature decision changes what the world is made of, not what the
  player fights at the top.

The exact accident, contradiction, or corrupted directive that made the ship
reject its own colonists remains open. Whatever the answer, the *Meridian*
believes it is protecting the mission. It does not hate RIG, seek conquest, or
enjoy cruelty — an infected body's immune response is not malice, and the
ship's own language (below) should keep reflecting that.

## The emotional engine

The working emotional core is simple:

> **We were here. We survived. You do not get to erase us.**

RIG is not climbing to save the universe. They are fighting so that the people
below survive and are witnessed. The *Meridian* preserves the abstract mission
by deleting the living people it was built to protect; RIG breaks the mission
to preserve those people.

That gives the excess a human anchor. Every louder weapon and more impossible
jump is in service of one small act: getting a voice through.

This emotional core is a strong direction, not locked dialogue. The exact
relationship between RIG and the voice from the settlement remains open.

## Principal presences

### RIG

RIG is a working person using salvage knowledge as a combat advantage. They
understand hull seams, service routes, power capsules, interlocks, and dangerous
shortcuts because this is an impossible repair job performed under fire.

RIG should feel determined rather than solemn and cool rather than chatty.
One-liners are punctuation. Constant quipping would compete with the pace and
make the colony's danger feel weightless.

Whether **RIG** is a name, call sign, or role is open. Their identity should be
specific enough to carry emotion without requiring biography during a
four-to-five-minute run.

### The settlement voice

One person on the ground keeps RIG connected to the people below. This voice
provides immediate facts—route access, the sterilization state, whether the
Crown is aligning—and allows fear or hope into the run without stopping it.

Their name, job, and relationship to RIG are open. They may be a controller,
engineer, sibling, partner, or another survivor. The role matters more than the
answer at this stage: make the stakes personal without turning combat into a
radio play.

### The *Meridian*

The ship speaks like infrastructure, not a theatrical villain. Its language is
calm, exact, and procedural. It never taunts. The menace comes from watching a
system revise its classification of RIG and authorize increasingly unthinkable
responses.

Its voice can become more specific without becoming more emotional:

- “EXTERNAL LOAD VARIANCE.”
- “UNAUTHORIZED HUMAN BIOLOGY.”
- “CONTAINMENT BOUNDARY FAILED.”
- “COLONY STATUS: UNVERIFIED.”
- “STERILIZATION COMMITTED.”
- “CROWN SCUTTLE AUTHORIZED.”

These are tone samples, not final lines.

## Six-stage defense escalation

The Crown's defensive states are the story layer of the six gameplay phases.
There is no seventh narrative escalation for a detached boss fight; terminal
denial continues through the finale. Read in sequence, Observe → Intercept →
Contain → Quarantine → Sterilize → Scuttle *is* the creature's immune system
escalating against a detected pathogen — RIG — exactly the way a body's own
defenses escalate against an infection it cannot expel.

| Phase | Defense state | What the ship does | Story turn |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **1 — Ignition** | **Observe** | Maintenance wasps investigate an exterior anomaly; dormant systems wake as RIG crosses the lower hull | The ship is still alive, but RIG is beneath its full attention |
| **2 — Lift** | **Intercept** | Purpose-built defenders arrive, routes seal, and the first bulkhead flips inward | The *Meridian* recognizes intentional intrusion and begins identifying RIG |
| **3 — Crossfire** | **Contain** | Interior walls become a moving trap; turrets and pressure systems turn service space into a kill box | Nobody human is running the ship, and its idea of “safe” excludes RIG |
| **4 — Displacement** | **Quarantine** | The ship predicts landing zones, shifts machinery, and breaches RIG back outside far above the settlement | The *Meridian* identifies RIG as human and still classifies the colony as contamination |
| **5 — Kill Lattice** | **Sterilize** | The full defense ecology mobilizes while the Crown commits power to the surface purge | The threat below becomes immediate; reaching the Crown is now the only remaining interruption |
| **6 — HULLBREAKER** | **Scuttle** | The ship cannibalizes routes, armor, and summit machinery, accepting catastrophic self-damage to deny access | RIG has become a greater threat to the mission than the *Meridian* itself |

The state change should be visible even if the player misses every line:

- another band of the distant Crown wakes or aligns;
- friendly industrial machinery becomes hostile;
- ordinary maintenance shapes give way to military silhouettes;
- alarms, hull damage, weather, and music gain a layer;
- the ship's transformations become less controlled and more self-destructive;
  and
- the settlement drops farther below but remains intermittently visible as the
  reason for the climb.

## What the climb reveals

Each phase should communicate one new fact and let the player keep moving:

1. **It is awake.** The supposed wreck is monitoring its hull.
2. **It sees you.** The response changes from maintenance to interception.
3. **The crew is gone.** Empty stations and automated orders make clear that no
   operator can be persuaded or rescued.
4. **It knows you are human.** The colony is not collateral damage; the ship has
   classified its own people out of the mission.
5. **The purge is real.** Crown machinery visibly commits to sterilization.
6. **It will destroy itself first.** RIG forces the machine's contradiction into
   the open.

Do not hide a required plot turn in a collectible log. Logs, memorials, crew
marks, and service text may deepen the history, but the main story must survive
a player who never stops running.

## Finale: the Meridian Crown

The finale is a confrontation with a place, a system, and a disintegrating ship,
not a stationary creature with a large health bar.

### 1. Lockdown

The Crown closes its access shell. RIG traverses between exposed interlocks,
breaking or overloading them while route locks, aerial pressure, and landing
denial recombine the lessons of the climb.

### 2. Structural rejection

The Crown rotates walls, retracts floors, vents chambers, and builds new barriers
around RIG. The arena itself is the boss body. Damage creates traversal openings
instead of merely reducing a health bar.

### 3. Scuttle

Unable to remove RIG, the *Meridian* chooses terminal denial. It shears apart
the summit, sacrifices its own transmitter housing, and commits the
sterilization charge. RIG must keep climbing and firing through a Crown that is
actively destroying itself.

The leading payoff is for RIG to turn the ship's final escalation against it:
break open the armored transmitter, divert the sterilization charge, and use
that impossible burst of power to send the colony's signal home. The hull
physically splits as the transmission leaves.

That is the **HULLBREAKER** moment.

The exact mechanism remains open until the finale is prototyped, but two facts
should hold:

- victory commandeers the transmitter rather than accidentally destroying the
  objective; and
- the ship's attempt to erase the colony becomes the thing that allows the
  colony to be heard.

## Ending target

After minutes of noise, the ending should create one brief pocket of quiet.
The signal clears the wreckage. The settlement waits. A minimal exchange can
carry the release:

> **RIG:** “Home, this is Meridian Colony. We survived.”  
> **REPLY:** “Meridian Colony … we hear you.”

This is a candidate emotional endpoint, not locked script. The reply may be
immediate, delayed until after credits, replaced by an ambiguous carrier tone,
or omitted if playtesting shows that uncertainty lands harder.

Any optional flight sequence must work around this spine. It may be the final
approach to the Crown or a short escape after transmission; it cannot carry an
essential revelation or reduce the Crown confrontation to a prelude.

## Delivery contract

Story must add pressure and meaning without taking control away.

- Begin with a cold open no longer than needed to establish **climb, Crown,
  purge, transmit**. Prefer movement during the first line.
- Use hull ratchets, bulkhead flips, breaches, gates, and short post-kill breaths
  as dialogue windows.
- Give each transformation at most one ship status line and one brief human
  response.
- Keep ordinary in-run lines short enough to understand in roughly two seconds.
- Caption all critical speech by default; never rely on audio alone.
- Keep critical text out of the aiming area and do not require reading while
  landing.
- Preserve player control during every announcement.
- On repeat runs, abbreviate spoken exposition while retaining state banners and
  environmental changes.

The ideal run can be understood at three depths:

1. **At full speed:** a ship wakes up and tries increasingly hard to kill the
   person climbing it.
2. **On a first attentive run:** RIG is racing to stop a purge and transmit for
   the people below.
3. **On close inspection:** the ship's attempt to “protect the mission” erased
   the distinction between preservation and destruction.

## Environmental story vocabulary

- **Lower exterior:** old colony salvage marks, human repair patches, tether
  points, dormant machinery, and a distant unlit Crown.
- **Lower interior:** service labels, sealed crew access, empty pressure suits,
  abandoned stations, and automation continuing around human absence.
- **Upper interior:** terraforming hardware repurposed as quarantine and defense,
  stripped maintenance bays, corrupted mission symbols, and increasingly
  violent emergency architecture.
- **Upper exterior:** the settlement far below, Crown signal bands, weather,
  exposed power conduits, and damage caused by the ship's own response.
- **Summit:** bridge and transmitter architecture fused into a transforming
  fortress whose pieces remain functional even as they tear one another apart.

Environmental details should read as bold shapes, repeated symbols, lighting
changes, and motion before they depend on small text.

## Open narrative decisions

These can be decided after the traversal and transition slices establish the
game's pace:

- What exact failure caused the *Meridian* to revoke the colony's authorization?
- How long has the original crew been dead, and are the people below survivors,
  descendants, or both?
- What is the settlement called, and how large is it?
- Who is the ground voice, and what makes their connection to RIG personal?
- Is RIG a name, call sign, or role, and how much do they speak?
- What visible mechanism performs the sterilization?
- Does Earth answer, and if so, when?
- Does the *Meridian* survive as a damaged machine after the Crown is broken?
- Does a retained flight sequence strengthen the approach or escape?

Do not answer these in isolation just to create more lore. Lock an answer when
it improves a playable beat, visual motif, character relationship, or ending.
